Whose church is it?

Almost every old church in Western Europe belongs to someone: a saint, the Virgin, Christ or the Trinity. I sorted {{n_churches|int}} church buildings in twelve countries by their patron to see what that geography looks like, and added Ireland's holy wells.

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · October 2026 · Data: Wikidata (CC0)

Walk into a village almost anywhere from Galway to Calabria and the church will usually carry a name: Saint Martin, Saint Peter, Notre-Dame, San Rocco, Sankt Nikolaus. These dedications were chosen over many centuries, for relics, for founders, for local bishops and for fashion, and once given they tended to stick. Put them on one map and you get a mosaic of Europe's saints: some everywhere, some almost nowhere outside one valley.

Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, lists {{n_churches|int}} church buildings with coordinates in the United Kingdom, Ireland, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Germany, Switzerland, Austria, Italy, Spain and Portugal. Only {{n_p825|int}} of them record their dedication in a structured field. For the rest I read the saint from the church's name, in eight languages, and checked that reading against the structured field wherever both exist. They agree {{validation.rules_plus_heldout_aliases.ALL.share|pct0}} of the time. In all, {{n_identified|int}} churches ({{share_identified|pct0}}) have an identifiable dedication.

02 · First of all, MaryThe Virgin outnumbers every saint

Mary is the most common dedication in every one of the twelve countries and leads in {{hex20.lead_any_cells.0.share|pct0}} of the hexagons with enough data. Her share rises as you go south: {{by_country.PT.share_mary|pct0}} of identified churches in Portugal, {{by_country.IT.share_mary|pct0}} in Italy and {{by_country.ES.share_mary|pct0}} in Spain, against {{by_country.GB.share_mary|pct0}} in the United Kingdom, {{by_country.DE.share_mary|pct0}} in Germany and {{by_country.LU.share_mary|pct0}} in Luxembourg. I have counted every Marian title as one: Notre-Dame, Our Lady of Mount Carmel, the Assumption, Mariä Himmelfahrt, Nuestra Señora del Pilar and the rest.

03 · Peter, John and MartinThe three great patrons

Set Mary aside and three names take most of the map. Peter leads in {{hex20.lead_saint_cells.0.share|pct0}} of hexagons, John in {{hex20.lead_saint_cells.1.share|pct0}} and Martin in {{hex20.lead_saint_cells.2.share|pct0}}. ('John' here is the Baptist and the Evangelist together, because a name such as Johanniskirche or St John's rarely says which.) Peter is strong almost everywhere and peaks in {{selected.Peter.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Peter.top_regions.0.share|pct0}} of churches). John runs through northern England and western Germany, with the highest shares in {{selected.John.top_regions.0.name}} and {{selected.John.top_regions.1.name}}.

Martin of Tours, the soldier who cut his cloak in half for a beggar, is the saint of France. With {{selected.Martin.by_country.FR|pct1}} of French churches he is second only to Peter among the saints, and his heartland is the north: {{selected.Martin.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Martin.top_regions.0.share|pct0}}), {{selected.Martin.top_regions.1.name}} ({{selected.Martin.top_regions.1.share|pct0}}) and {{selected.Martin.top_regions.2.name}} ({{selected.Martin.top_regions.2.share|pct0}}). Across the Channel he falls to {{selected.Martin.by_country.GB|pct1}}.

04 · Saints with a homeRegional patrons

Some saints are a map of one region's history. The early saints of Ireland, Wales, Scotland, Cornwall and Brittany (a group of {{celtic_n|raw}} names, listed in the methods) account for {{by_country.IE.share_celtic|pct0}} of Irish churches and {{by_country.GB.share_celtic|pct1}} of British ones, peaking in {{selected.Celtic saints.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Celtic saints.top_regions.0.share|pct0}}) and {{selected.Celtic saints.top_regions.1.name}} ({{selected.Celtic saints.top_regions.1.share|pct0}}). In France they are {{selected.Celtic saints.by_country.FR|pct1}} of churches overall but {{celtic_bretagne|pct0}} in Brittany.

Nicholas, patron of sailors and merchants, is strongest along the North Sea and Baltic coasts: {{selected.Nicholas.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Nicholas.top_regions.0.share|pct0}}), {{selected.Nicholas.top_regions.1.name}} and {{selected.Nicholas.top_regions.2.name}}. Lambert, the bishop of Maastricht killed at Liège, belongs to the Low Countries, peaking in {{selected.Lambert.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Lambert.top_regions.0.share|pct1}}). Boniface, killed near Dokkum, has his highest share in {{selected.Boniface.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Boniface.top_regions.0.share|pct1}}); Kilian, martyred at Würzburg, in {{selected.Kilian.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Kilian.top_regions.0.share|pct1}}); Ambrose of Milan in {{selected.Ambrose.top_regions.0.name}} ({{selected.Ambrose.top_regions.0.share|pct1}}) and {{selected.Ambrose.top_regions.1.name}} ({{selected.Ambrose.top_regions.1.share|pct1}}). These are small shares, but they are almost nowhere else.

To find each region's own saint, I looked for the saint with at least eight churches in a region whose share there most exceeds its share in the rest of the same country. 05 · The road to SantiagoJames near his shrine

By tradition the apostle James is buried at Santiago de Compostela, the goal of one of medieval Europe's great pilgrimages. In Spain, his share of churches does fall away with distance from the cathedral: {{james.bands.ES.0.share|pct1}} of churches within 150 km (mostly Galicia), {{james.bands.ES.2.share|pct1}} at 300 to 500 km and {{james.bands.ES.3.share|pct1}} at 500 to 750 km. Galicia as a whole has {{james.galicia_share|pct1}}, against {{james.spain_share|pct1}} for Spain. Beyond Spain the gradient is faint: France's share drifts from {{james.bands.FR.0.share|pct1}} in the south-west to {{james.bands.FR.2.share|pct1}} beyond 1,000 km, and Britain, at {{selected.James.by_country.GB|pct1}}, has more of him than France. Pilgrim roads may have spread his cult, but they do not leave a clear trail in church names.

06 · Michael on the heights?A test in Britain and Ireland

Michael the archangel is often said to favour high places: Mont-Saint-Michel, St Michael's Mount, the tower on Glastonbury Tor. I tested this where I have terrain data, in Britain and Ireland. Churches of St Michael do stand on higher ground overall (median {{michael.median_elev_michael|f0}} m above sea level against {{michael.median_elev_other|f0}} m for other dedications), but they are not more often on a local high point. {{michael.share_top_michael|pct1}} of Michael's {{michael.n_michael|int}} churches stand higher than at least 90% of the ground within 1 km, against {{michael.share_top_other|pct1}} of other churches, and Michael ranks {{michael.rank_of_michael|raw}} of {{michael.n_ranked|raw}} dedications on that measure. The famous hilltop Michaels are real, but they are a small part of a large, mostly lowland cult.

07 · VarietyWhere every church has a different saint

A way to measure variety that is fair to regions of different size is to ask how many different dedications you would meet among 100 churches picked at random. The answer ranges from {{richest.0.richness100|f0}} in {{richest.0.name}} to {{poorest.9.richness100|f0}} in {{poorest.9.name}}. The Celtic west is the most varied place in Western Europe: {{richest.1.name}} ({{richest.1.richness100|f0}}) and {{richest.3.name}} ({{richest.3.richness100|f0}}) follow Cornwall, where many parishes keep the name of their own early saint. The least varied are scattered, from {{poorest.9.name}} and {{poorest.7.name}} to {{poorest.8.name}} and {{poorest.6.name}}, and mostly they are places where Mary or a few great saints take a large share. Across the {{n_regions_rich|raw}} regions with enough churches, variety and Mary's share have a correlation of {{richness_corr_mary|f2}}.

08 · Holy wellsBrigid's water

Wikidata holds {{wells.n|int}} holy wells in the study countries, {{wells.by_country.IE|int}} of them in Ireland, so this is really an Irish story (the British, French and Italian lists are short). Of the Irish wells, {{wells.ie_identified|int}} have a name I can read as a saint, and the top of that list looks quite unlike Ireland's churches. Brigid holds {{wells.ie_top.0.share|pct0}} of the named wells but only {{wells.ie_top.0.church_share|pct1}} of the churches; Mary {{wells.ie_top.1.share|pct0}} of wells against {{wells.ie_top.1.church_share|pct0}} of churches. The early Irish saints together hold {{wells.ie_celtic_share|pct0}} of named wells against {{wells.ie_church_celtic_share|pct0}} of churches. Wells are older, smaller, more local places of devotion, and Brigid is at home there.

09 · What the data can sayCoverage, country by country

Wikidata is uneven. It has {{by_country.IT.per_1000km2|f0}} church buildings per 1,000 km² for Italy but {{by_country.PT.per_1000km2|f0}} for Portugal and {{by_country.ES.per_1000km2|f0}} for Spain. The share I can assign to a saint ranges from {{by_country.IT.share_identified|pct0}} in Italy to {{by_country.CH.share_identified|pct0}} in Switzerland and {{by_country.DE.share_identified|pct0}} in Germany, where many Protestant churches carry only a village name. Belgium records {{by_country.BE.share_p825|pct0}} of dedications in the structured field; Germany {{by_country.DE.share_p825|pct0}}. That is why every figure on this page is a share within a country or region.

10 · MethodHow I did it

  1. Churches. Every Wikidata item classed as a church building with coordinates in the twelve countries, extracted per country, deduplicated by item and kept if it falls on the European mainland or islands (no Canaries, Azores, Madeira or overseas France): {{n_churches|int}} buildings. The list includes chapels, ruins and some former churches.
  2. Dedication. Where Wikidata records 'dedicated to' (P825) I use it ({{n_p825|int}} churches). Otherwise I read the name: saint prefixes in each language (St, Saint, Sainte, Sankt, Hl., Sint, San, Santa, Santo, Santi, Sant', São and more), French hyphenated forms (Saint-Martin, but not 'église de Saint-Rémy', which is a place), German and Dutch compounds (Martinskirche, Nikolaikirche, Marienkerk), and fixed titles (Notre-Dame, Unserer Lieben Frau, Onze-Lieve-Vrouw, Nuestra Señora, Nossa Senhora, Madonna, Sacré-Cœur, Holy Trinity, Santiago). A table of name forms maps the languages to one saint: Pierre, Pietro, Pedro, Petrus and Pieter are all Peter. It combines a hand-written core with the English, French, German, Spanish, Italian and Latin labels of all {{n_ded_items|int}} dedication items used in the data. Names the table does not know stand as their own saint.
  3. Name level. I group by name, not person: John is the Baptist and the Evangelist, James the Greater and the Less, Anthony of Padua and the Abbot. All Marian titles are Mary. The first saint named is the church's dedication; {{share_multi_dedication|pct1}} of identified churches name more than one.
  4. Checking. Of {{validation.core_rules.ALL.both|int}} churches with both P825 and a saint in their name, the name reader agrees with P825 on {{validation.core_rules.ALL.share|pct1}}. I then learned {{validation.n_aliases|raw}} aliases for names the rules did not know (such as Flemish Kwintens for Quentin) from churches that have both; learned on one half of the churches and tested on the other, agreement is {{validation.rules_plus_heldout_aliases.ALL.share|pct1}}.
  5. Maps and regions. Hexagons are 20 km from centre to corner in the European LAEA projection. Regions are NUTS 2016 level 2 (Eurostat GISCO). Variety is rarefied richness at 100 churches. Signature saints need at least eight churches in the region.
  6. Michael and height. Ground height from AWS Terrain Tiles (zoom 10) at each church in Britain and Ireland, compared with 24 points 500 m and 1 km away.
  7. Holy wells. {{wells.n|int}} Wikidata holy wells in the study countries; dedication from P825 or the name (St X's Well, Lady's Well, and Irish Tobar Mhuire, Tobar Bhríde and Tobar Phádraig forms).

Caveats

This is a map of Wikidata, not a census of churches: coverage and the use of P825 differ by country, region and even by editor. Reading saints from names is right about {{validation.rules_plus_heldout_aliases.ALL.share|pct0}} of the time where it can be checked, and probably less well for rare local saints. A dedication can change over the centuries, and a modern name may not be the medieval one. Ties between saints in small hexagons are broken by the saint's overall frequency. The Celtic saints group is my own list of early medieval saints of Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall and Brittany ({{celtic_n|raw}} names); continental missionaries of Irish birth such as Columbanus, Gall, Fiacre and Kilian are not in it.
